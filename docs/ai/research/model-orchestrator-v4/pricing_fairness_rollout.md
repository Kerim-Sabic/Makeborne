# Pricing fairness for routed AI work, and a statistically sound evaluation and rollout plan for a model-routing change (as of 23 October 2026)

Scope note: Makeborne context is credits at about $0.065 each, plans at $20/$49/$99 a month, and an orchestrator that tries cheap models or efforts first and escalates on failed checks, with Makeborne absorbing the cost of failed cheap attempts. Offline eval sets have 12 to 20 cases per task class. There is a blind pairwise Opus judge run in both orders, plus shadow mode. Labels used below: **[official]** means a vendor's own page, docs or blog. **[secondary]** means a third-party guide or press summary. **[anecdotal]** means individual user reports. **[computed]** means I calculated it here from standard formulas, not taken from a source. **[unverified]** means I could not confirm it against a primary source.

---

## Q1. How do AI builders price routed or variable-cost AI work, which charge for failed attempts or refund bad output, what backlash has there been, and do they show which model ran?

### Takeaway
The market has converged on cost-tracking credits, which bill by complexity, effort or tokens. On top of that sit a few patterns that make variable pricing feel fair: a discount for letting the vendor route ("Auto"), visibility after the fact into what each action cost and which model ran, and explicit free or refundable failure paths (Gamma charges nothing on errors; Framer has "Mark as Bad"; Readdy has Credit Care; Lovable and v0 give free fix quotas). Every major backlash (Cursor in June and July 2025, Replit with effort pricing and Agent 3 in 2025, the GPT-5 router in August 2025) came from bills or quality the user could not predict, or from changes that were badly communicated. None came from variable pricing as such. Makeborne's plan to absorb failed cheap attempts goes further than most competitors: Lovable, Replit and Base44 do not promise this in writing.

### Inferences
- Four mechanisms recur at the fair end of the market: (a) failures caused by the system cost nothing (Gamma, Adobe, Readdy, Framer, the Lovable and v0 fix quotas); (b) per-action cost can be seen afterwards (Lovable "Credits used", Replit hover, Gamma's image price shown in advance); (c) letting the vendor route is cheaper (Copilot 10–30%, Cursor Auto in Cost mode, Framer's 0.9x Sonnet); (d) spend controls (Replit alerts and budget caps). Makeborne could offer all four. Absorbing failed cheap attempts is a stronger promise than Lovable's (non-refundable credits plus 10 free fixes a day), Replit's (silent on failed work) or Base44's (support refusals reported). It is worth saying so in plain language on the pricing page.
- Recommended pricing principle: charge the user the price for the task class at the tier they were promised, quoted before the run when possible and capped. Do not charge the realized cost of whatever attempts happened. Cheap attempts that fail checks and the escalation overhead stay Makeborne's cost. This is the logic behind Cursor's Auto Cost mode, which bills as a Cursor-model request "whichever model serves it". It keeps routing risk off the user and avoids the Replit pattern of bills that jump without warning.
- Show which model or tier produced the final output (as Copilot does on hover, and as OpenAI promised after GPT-5), but do not bill per tier when routing is automatic. Price differences should come from the size or complexity of the task, not from which model the router picked. Otherwise every escalation looks like a hidden charge.
- Refund-for-bad-output needs guardrails. Readdy caps allowances (10 to 480 a month), v0 caps free fixes "to prevent abuse", and Lovable gives 10 a day. Uncapped "Mark as Bad" invites gaming, and when it fails quietly it causes the trust damage seen in Framer's forum posts. If Makeborne offers it, the refund should be immediate and shown in the ledger, with a visible monthly allowance.
- Trigger pattern behind the backlashes: changing price structure and capability at the same time (Replit's effort pricing followed by the more autonomous Agent 3) and wording that sounds unlimited while the real limits sit elsewhere (Cursor's "unlimited"). Remedies that worked or were promised: refund windows (Cursor 16 Jun–4 Jul), credits for everyone (Replit's $10, secondary), resetting limits (Anthropic, April 2026), and advance notice.
- Unit economics at Makeborne's price point [computed]: at $0.065 per credit, a $20 plan buys about 308 credits of value, $49 about 754 and $99 about 1,523. For comparison, Lovable converted Cloud balances at $0.25 per credit on Pro and $0.50 on Business (different credit units, so not directly comparable).

- Replit's official docs and blog do not say whether failed or rolled-back checkpoints are charged. The "charged for failed attempts" claim is a single Reddit report.
- Lovable's current plan prices were not on the docs page I read. Third-party figures put Pro at 100 credits and top-ups at $15 per 50, which is unverified against Lovable's own pricing page.
- Cursor's docs do not say whether the model Auto chose is shown. I also could not confirm the current Auto Cost per-token rate (the $1.25/$6 figures date from Sep 2025).
- Gamma's per-action credit costs beyond images are not published officially.
- I found no published data on how often refund-for-bad-output features are used or abused (Framer, Readdy, v0).

## Q2. What evaluation statistics apply to LLM systems: error bars, paired and clustered tests, Bradley-Terry, judge noise, position bias, minimum detectable effects at small n, sequential testing and non-inferiority?

### Takeaway
With 12 to 20 cases per task class, the offline set can only catch large regressions. A blind pairwise judge with n=20 decided pairs needs at least 15 of 20 wins (75%) for significance, and it has only about a 25% chance of detecting a true 65% win rate [computed]. The right framing for "the cheap route is no worse" is a paired non-inferiority test with a stated margin. It needs roughly 65 decided pairs for a 15-point win-rate margin and 150 for a 10-point margin [computed]. That means pooling across task classes, repeating samples per case (K>1), and clustering standard errors by case or project. A judge A-vs-A noise floor should be measured directly. The literature documents order flips and run-to-run inconsistency in judges but does not offer a standard number.

### Cited Findings

**Anthropic / Miller (2024), "Adding Error Bars to Evals" [official research]**
- The five recommendations: (1) compute standard errors with the CLT, since bootstrapping is unnecessary for simple means; (2) use clustered standard errors when questions come in related groups; (3) reduce variance by resampling answers per question (K) or using next-token probabilities; (4) analyse paired differences when two models answer the same questions; (5) run a power analysis. — [Anthropic: A statistical approach to model evaluations](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals); [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Paired SE = √(SE_A² + SE_B² − 2·SE_A·SE_B·Corr(s_A, s_B)), so positive correlation between models on the same items shrinks it. With correlation 0.5, variance falls from 1/6 to 1/9 (a third lower). — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Clustering: SE_clustered adds the within-cluster cross-products. Clustered SEs were 3.05x the naive ones on DROP, 1.88x on MGSM and 1.10x on RACE-H, so "ignoring clustering can understate uncertainty by up to about 3×". — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Resampling K answers per question (uniform-difficulty example): K=2 cuts variance by a third, K=4 by half and K=6 by 5/9, with a ceiling of 2/3. — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Sample size: n = (z_{α/2}+z_β)²(ω² + σ_A²/K_A + σ_B²/K_B)/δ². MDE: δ = (z_{α/2}+z_β)√[(ω² + σ_A²/K_A + σ_B²/K_B)/n]. Detecting a 3-point difference needs about 969 questions, and the paper suggests new evals have at least 1,000. With n=198, raising K from 1 to 10 lowers the MDE from 13.2% to 7.5%. — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Reporting: give n, the SE in parentheses, cluster counts, pairwise differences with their SEs and CIs, and score correlations. Do not lower temperature to cut variance unless that temperature is what you are studying. — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)

**Chatbot Arena / Bradley-Terry**
- Chatbot Arena fits Bradley-Terry with bootstrap CIs. A simulation in its appendix found bootstrap and sandwich-estimator intervals "often identical to the naked eye". It also includes a nonparametric extension for non-transitive preferences. — [Chiang et al., arXiv 2403.04132](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.04132)
- Standard practice: resample votes with replacement, refit Bradley-Terry each time, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. — [Evalica, arXiv 2412.11314](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.11314)
- Fragility: dropping just a handful of preferences can change the top-ranked model on some arenas. — [arXiv 2508.11847](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.11847)
- LMSYS reportedly moved from online Elo to batch Bradley-Terry in December 2023 because online Elo intervals were too wide. — [aiwiki.ai](https://www.aiwiki.ai/wiki/elo_rating_system_ai/raw) [secondary]

**Judge position bias and noise**
- "Judging the Judges" (Shi et al.): 12 to 15 judges, MT-Bench and DevBench, more than 100,000 instances. It defines position consistency, preference fairness and repetition stability. Position bias "is not due to random variation", varies by judge and task, and is stronger on items that are hard to judge. — [arXiv 2406.07791](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07791v9)
- MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) introduced the swap-order check. GPT-4's swap consistency is often cited at about 65%. — [Zheng et al., arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685) [the 65% figure comes from secondary summaries; not confirmed in the paper text here]
- A 2026 study of mitigations separates position bias from position sensitivity. Swap-based tie handling helps judges that often reverse their verdict, and on LLMBar's adversarial items position swapping hurt every model. — [arXiv 2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.23178)
- Judges give different verdicts on identical prompts because of non-greedy decoding ("flipping noise"). Earlier work often wrongly assumed judges were deterministic. — [arXiv 2408.13006](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13006v1)
- Naive win-rate estimates from noisy judges can be badly biased, with coverage that "varies dramatically with the magnitude and direction of judge bias". The paper proposes corrections that use human-labelled calibration data (prediction-powered inference, Rogan–Gladen). — [arXiv 2601.05420 (ICML 2026)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.05420)
- Practitioner reports: an 18% verdict flip rate from swapping order alone (1,200 pairs, gpt-4o), which fell below 4% when wins required agreement in both orders and disagreements counted as ties. Another report saw a 61% win rate drop to 46% when the same 400 pairs were swapped. — [dev.to (Chen)](https://dev.to/marcuswwchen/position-bias-in-llm-as-judge-flipped-18-of-our-verdicts-13lf); [dev.to (Andersson)](https://dev.to/maya_andersson_dev/your-llm-as-judge-has-a-position-bias-you-are-not-measuring-k5l) [anecdotal]
- Lovable on judge validity: one LLM judge "was recalibrated after ranking a hollow build near the top". Lovable runs each build more than once because "one beautiful result can be luck", and human judgment is the final check. — [Lovable blog](https://lovable.dev/blog/the-model-picker-is-a-dead-end)
- GitHub routinely audits its LLM judge and says keeping it aligned with human reviewers is hard. — [GitHub blog, 17 Jan 2025](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)

**Sequential testing**
- Always-valid p-values via mSPRT let you look at results continuously without inflating type-I error. Optimizely has used them since 2015 (its "New Stats Engine"). — [Johari, Pekelis, Walsh, arXiv 1512.04922](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.04922); [Analytics-Toolkit](https://blog.analytics-toolkit.com/?p=1524)
- Repeatedly peeking at ordinary fixed-horizon p-values under a true null "routinely" pushes false-positive rates above 25%. The trade-off is that mSPRT has less power per sample than a fixed-horizon test. — [tessl peeking reference](https://tessl.io/registry/testland/peeking-problem-reference) [secondary]

**Non-inferiority and guardrails**
- Spotify's guardrail framework tests guardrails for non-inferiority against a margin (the NIM) and adds an inferiority (deterioration) test to catch margins set too wide. Guardrails tested with non-inferiority tests do not need a multiplicity-adjusted α, but the type-II rate must be corrected for the desired power. "The treatment can be non-inferior to control while it is also inferior to control." — [Schultzberg et al. (Spotify), arXiv 2402.11609](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11609)
- Non-inferiority asks "is the variant significantly worse?" with a "margin of caring". Choosing the null changes the decision even on identical data. — [Analytics-Toolkit](https://blog.analytics-toolkit.com/2017/case-non-inferiority-designs-ab-testing/)
- Kohavi, Tang and Xu separate organizational guardrails (their Ch. 7) from trust guardrails, mainly sample-ratio mismatch (SRM, Ch. 21). SRM invalidates an experiment until its root cause is found, and logging bugs are the most common cause. A/A tests validate the tooling. — [Cambridge: Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments, SRM chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/trustworthy-online-controlled-experiments/sample-ratio-mismatch-and-other-trustrelated-guardrail-metrics/8DBB0F59AC7729D7BC6B94690DB9CCD5); [Speero on A/A tests](https://speero.com/post/when-to-run-an-a-a-test-and-when-to-check-instrumentation)
- A checklist attributed to Kohavi says to "always include a quality guardrail (latency / error); it is the most-missed category". The claim that about 8% of experiments are invalid because of SRM is unverified. — [tessl A/B validity checklist](https://tessl.io/registry/testland/ab-test-validity-checklist) [secondary]

**Power and MDE at Makeborne's sample sizes [computed]**

These come from an exact binomial sign test on decided pairs (both-order agreement, ties dropped), two-sided α=0.05:

| decided pairs n | wins needed | power at true win rate 0.65 | 0.70 | 0.75 | 0.80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 10 (83%) | 0.15 | 0.25 | 0.39 | 0.56 |
| 16 | 13 (81%) | 0.13 | 0.25 | 0.40 | 0.60 |
| 20 | 15 (75%) | 0.25 | 0.42 | 0.62 | 0.80 |
| 40 | 27 (68%) | 0.44 | 0.70 | 0.90 | 0.98 |
| 60 | 39 (65%) | 0.56 | 0.84 | 0.97 | 1.00 |
| 100 | 61 (61%) | 0.83 | 0.98 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 200 | 115 (57%) | 0.99 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

- For 80% power at α=0.05 two-sided (normal approximation), detecting a true win rate of 0.55 takes about 783 decided pairs, 0.60 about 194, 0.65 about 85 and 0.70 about 47.
- Non-inferiority on judge preference (H0: the new route's win rate among decided pairs ≤ 0.5−m; true rate 0.5; one-sided α=0.05; power 0.8) takes 614 decided pairs for m=5pp, 150 for m=10pp, 65 for m=15pp and 34 for m=20pp.
- Paired non-inferiority on a binary pass rate (McNemar-type approximation, true difference 0) depends on the discordance rate ψ, the share of tasks where the arms disagree. At ψ=0.10 it takes 687 tasks for a 3pp margin, 247 for 5pp and 62 for 10pp. At ψ=0.05 it takes 124 tasks for 5pp. Unpaired designs need far more: at a 0.85 baseline and a 5pp margin, about 631 per arm.
- A/A noise band: even an unbiased judge comparing two identical-quality arms gives a 95% CI half-width on the win rate of ±28pp at n=12, ±22pp at n=20, ±14pp at n=50 and ±10pp at n=100.
- Zero-failure bound (rule of three): 0 failures in 20 cases bounds the true failure rate only below about 14% (exact 13.9%). Zero in 100 bounds it below 3%, and zero in 300 below 1%.

### Inferences
- On 12 to 20 cases, the blind pairwise judge works as a smoke alarm for big regressions, not as a way to prove quality is equal. Gates should be framed as "no catastrophic loss" checks (for example, the new route loses at most n_loss decided pairs) plus deterministic pass-rate checks. "Not significantly worse" should never be read as "equal".
- To reach decision-grade power without many more hand-written cases: (1) run each case K=3 to 5 times per arm at production temperature (Miller: K=4 roughly halves per-item variance); (2) pool across task classes with case or project as the cluster and report clustered SEs; (3) use paired analysis throughout, since every case runs under both arms; (4) add shadow-mode production traffic as a third, larger paired set (see Q3).
- Measure the judge's own noise floor before trusting any win rate. Run A-vs-A, with the same route sampled twice, in both orders, over the eval set. Report (a) the order-flip rate, (b) the tie rate and (c) the A/A win-rate spread. Any candidate effect inside the A/A band is noise. Judge calibration should be checked on a small human-labelled subset, as Lovable and GitHub both audit their judges, and arXiv 2601.05420 shows how a few human labels can debias win-rate estimates.
- Both-order agreement (a win counts only when both orders agree, otherwise a tie) is the accepted position-bias control and matches Makeborne's current setup. Ties should be reported, not dropped silently, since a high tie rate is itself a warning sign about the judge.
- For online decisions, use always-valid or group-sequential monitoring for guardrails, so that daily dashboard checks don't inflate false alarms. Use fixed-horizon, pre-registered non-inferiority tests for the primary quality metric.

### Gaps
- I found no published standard value for an LLM-judge A-vs-A noise floor. It must be measured per judge and per task.
- I could not confirm the GPT-4 ~65% swap-consistency figure in the MT-Bench paper text itself (it comes from secondary summaries).
- I did not find Kohavi-authored guidance on choosing a non-inferiority margin for LLM quality metrics specifically.
- I found no Eppo- or Statsig-specific documentation on sequential methods in this pass.

## Q3. How should a routing change be rolled out (shadow mode, canary, soak, online quality metrics, guardrails, kill switches), and how did Anthropic, Cursor, Lovable, GitHub and OpenAI handle theirs?

### Takeaway
The strongest recent evidence is Anthropic's 23 April 2026 postmortem. Three product-layer changes, one of them a lower default reasoning effort chosen for speed, each passed internal evals and dogfooding but together degraded Claude Code for about six weeks. Anthropic now commits to soak periods, a broader eval suite, ablations and gradual rollouts for "changes that could trade off against intelligence". That is exactly the category a cheap-first router falls into. Industry practice (GitHub's swappable model proxy and internal canaries, Cursor's online accept-rate loop, Lovable's "models earn their traffic", OpenAI's GPT-5 router failure) points to staged traffic ramps watched with behavioural quality metrics, a kill switch that can revert instantly, and showing users which model ran.

### Cited Findings

**Anthropic, 23 April 2026 postmortem [official]**
- Change 1 (4 Mar to 7 Apr 2026): Claude Code's default reasoning effort went from high to medium "to reduce long waits that made the UI appear frozen". Internal evals showed only a slight loss in intelligence, but users found it noticeably less intelligent, and Anthropic later called it the wrong tradeoff. Opus 4.7 now defaults to xhigh and other models to high. — [Anthropic engineering postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem)
- Change 2 (26 Mar to 10 Apr): a bug cleared old reasoning on every turn instead of once for sessions idle over an hour. It passed code review, unit and end-to-end tests, automated verification and dogfooding, and was hidden by two unrelated changes. Root cause took over a week to confirm. — [Anthropic engineering postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem)
- Change 3 (16 to 20 Apr): a verbosity-capping prompt (25 words between tool calls, 100 words for final responses) showed no regressions in weeks of internal testing. Later ablations on a broader eval set found a 3% drop for Opus 4.6 and 4.7. — [Anthropic engineering postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem)
- Why it was hard to see: each change hit "a different traffic slice on a different schedule", so the combined effect looked like "broad, inconsistent degradation". Early reports were hard to separate from normal feedback variation. — [Anthropic engineering postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem)
- Commitments: staff will use the exact public build; a broad suite of per-model evals will run on every system-prompt change, with continued ablations; model-specific changes will be gated to their target model; changes that could trade off against intelligence get soak periods, a broader eval suite and gradual rollouts; and usage limits were reset for all subscribers on 23 April. — [Anthropic engineering postmortem](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem)
- Secondary coverage confirms the dates and fixes (v2.1.116 by 20 April). A claimed "33%" degradation figure in one Spanish article has no corroboration. — [InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/05/anthropic-claude-code-postmortem); [ecosistemastartup.com](https://ecosistemastartup.com/anthropic-revela-3-errores-que-degradaron-claude-33/) [the 33% figure is unverified]

**GitHub Copilot [official]**
- GitHub runs more than 4,000 offline tests, built around about 100 containerized repositories that pass CI, are deliberately broken, and must be repaired, plus over 1,000 technical chat questions. The same tests run daily against production models, and a decline triggers an audit. Online, "live internal evaluations work like canary tests": internal users are switched to the new model, for all major changes. A proxy lets GitHub swap the backend model without client changes. The adoption metrics are acceptance rate and latency, which trade off, since higher latency can raise acceptance by showing fewer suggestions. — [GitHub blog, 17 Jan 2025](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-we-evaluate-models-for-github-copilot/)
- The completions team went beyond acceptance rate to accepted-and-retained characters, because optimizing acceptance alone favoured short, simple suggestions. The new model had a 12% higher acceptance rate. — [GitHub blog: The road to better completions](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/the-road-to-better-completions-building-a-faster-smarter-github-copilot-with-a-new-custom-model/) [from a search snippet; full text not read]
- A/B tests run on a small share of real traffic against a control group, and offline results decide what gets tested online. — [ZenML LLMOps database summary of a GitHub talk](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/building-production-evaluation-systems-for-github-copilot-at-scale) [secondary]
- Auto can serve "evaluation models" to individual-plan users, with an opt-out. — [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/auto-model-selection)

**Cursor [official]**
- The Tab model serves over 400 million requests a day. Cursor rolls out new checkpoints often, taking 1.5 to 2 hours to roll one out and collect data for the next step. The new model makes 21% fewer suggestions with a 28% higher accept rate. — [Cursor blog: Tab RL](https://cursor.com/blog/tab-rl) [vendor-reported]
- After the pricing incident, Cursor promised advance notice of pricing changes and usage visibility. — [Cursor blog](https://cursor.com/blog/june-2025-pricing)

**Lovable [official]**
- Candidate models are tested inside Lovable's own agent loop, with its prompts, tools and context, not judged on public leaderboards alone. Builds are run more than once. Signals include human judgment, LLM judges and expected rankings, and builds are inspected when the signals disagree. "Models that consistently produce better apps earn more work", and Lovable's in-house models must also "earn their traffic". Switching models mid-build costs history compression and rebuilt caches, so a switch must be "worth the cost". In one eval, a frontier model was 15% faster, took 40% fewer turns and scored 2 to 3% higher. The post does not mention A/B tests, shadow traffic or rollout gating. — [Lovable blog, 11 Aug 2026](https://lovable.dev/blog/the-model-picker-is-a-dead-end)

**OpenAI GPT-5 router [press]**
- The autoswitcher failed for part of launch day, so GPT-5 seemed weaker than intended. OpenAI's fixes: adjust the decision boundary, make it more transparent which model answers, bring back legacy models, and add Auto, Fast and Thinking modes so users can bypass the router. Removing models without notice drew criticism. — [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/openai-returns-old-models-to-chatgpt-as-sam-altman-admits-bumpy-gpt-5-rollout); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/12/chatgpts-model-picker-is-back-and-its-complicated)

**Replit [official + press]**
- The pricing model rolled out immediately to new users and from 1 July 2025 to existing subscribers. The worst cost complaints came after the more autonomous Agent 3 shipped in September 2025, because a capability change multiplied the effect of the earlier pricing change. — [Replit blog](https://replit.com/blog/effort-based-pricing); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/09/18/replit_agent3_pricing/)

**Signals for online quality metrics (from vendor product mechanics)**
- Explicit "bad" labels: Framer's "Mark as Bad" doubles as feedback "used to improve the product". — [Framer blog](https://www.framer.com/blog/ai-credits-simpler-plans-and-lower-prices/)
- Discard and revert events: Readdy's discard reverts the project. Lovable's FAQ tells users to revert to an earlier version when a fix keeps failing. Gamma notes that undo does not return credits, which shows undo is a tracked event. — [Readdy docs](https://docs.readdy.ai/features/credit-care); [Lovable FAQ](https://docs.lovable.dev/introduction/faq); [Gamma Help](https://help.gamma.app/en/articles/7834324-how-do-ai-credits-work-in-gamma)
- Accept and retain: Cursor's accept rate and GitHub's accepted-and-retained characters (above).
- Fix-loop depth: Lovable advises using "Try to fix" "once or twice, then investigate", which implies that repeated fix attempts per error signal a failure. — [Lovable FAQ](https://docs.lovable.dev/introduction/faq)
- Kohavi-style trust guardrails: SRM, plus a quality guardrail for latency and errors. — [Cambridge SRM chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/trustworthy-online-controlled-experiments/sample-ratio-mismatch-and-other-trustrelated-guardrail-metrics/8DBB0F59AC7729D7BC6B94690DB9CCD5); [tessl checklist](https://tessl.io/registry/testland/ab-test-validity-checklist) [secondary]

### Inferences
- A cheap-first router is the kind of change Anthropic now soaks: like its high-to-medium effort switch, it trades intelligence for cost or latency. That lowered default passed internal evals and still read as "dumber" in production. Makeborne should assume offline wins on 12 to 20 cases will not catch subtle losses. A proposed staged plan, built from the sources above:
  1. **Offline gate:** both-order blind judge plus deterministic checks, K=3 to 5 samples per case, judge A/A noise floor measured first, and a "no large regression" rule per task class (for example, the new route loses no more decided pairs than the A/A band allows). Results pooled across classes with clustered SEs.
  2. **Shadow:** run the cheap route on live traffic alongside the current route, serve only the current output, and log check pass/fail, escalation rate, cost and latency. Judge a paired sample of shadow outputs offline to reach the ~65 to 150 decided pairs needed for a 15pp or 10pp non-inferiority margin [computed]. Shadowing doubles inference cost, so sample it (for example, 10 to 20% of jobs).
  3. **Internal canary:** staff and friendly accounts first, as GitHub does with internal users and Anthropic's commitment to use the exact public build.
  4. **Gradual exposure:** for example 1% → 5% → 25% → 50% → 100%, randomized per project, not per request, so a project's builds stay consistent and outcomes stay independent. Check SRM at every stage.
  5. **Soak:** hold each stage long enough to see delayed signals (reverts, regenerations, "Mark as bad", refunds, churn or downgrades). A minimum of 7 days at the largest stage covers weekly usage cycles. This is a judgment call, as no source gives a number.
  6. **Kill switch:** a single flag that sends every task class back to the previous route instantly (GitHub's proxy pattern), which Makeborne's off/shadow/on modes already support. Add automatic rollback when a guardrail is breached.
- Online metrics to pre-register, each with a non-inferiority margin. Primary (quality): the share of jobs completed without the user reverting, regenerating, discarding or marking as bad within N minutes, and follow-up prompts per completed artifact (re-prompt rate). Secondary: escalation rate, check-failure rate by tier, credits charged per job, Makeborne's realized cost per job, p50 and p95 latency, publish or export rate. Guardrails: error and timeout rate, latency, refund and support-ticket rate, SRM. Use always-valid or sequential monitoring on guardrails, and a fixed horizon for the primary.
- Never change pricing and routing in the same release. Replit's experience shows how a capability change multiplied the effect of an earlier pricing change. Announce billing changes in advance (Cursor's commitment) and offer a goodwill remedy, such as a refund window or credit reset, if a rollout goes wrong (Cursor, Anthropic, Replit).
- Show the tier or model that ran (Copilot shows it on hover, and OpenAI promised to after GPT-5). Consider a "use best model" override like OpenAI's Fast and Thinking, so users who distrust the router can bypass it. That reduces backlash risk even if few use it.

### Gaps
- No vendor I found publishes its exact soak durations, ramp percentages or rollback thresholds. Anthropic commits to "soak periods" and "gradual rollouts" without numbers.
- I found no public data from Lovable, Bolt, v0, Base44 or Gamma on online quality metrics (undo, re-prompt or revert rates) used to gate model changes. The metric suggestions above are inferred from product mechanics.
- I found no public description of shadow-mode use by these AI builders for model routing specifically. Shadow mode is standard practice in ML deployment, but I did not find a sourced industry example in this pass.
- I did not read the GitHub completions post in full. The accepted-and-retained-characters detail comes from a search snippet.
